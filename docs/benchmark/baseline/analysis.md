# Baseline run — statistical reading

120 matches at 3x, 30 per matchup, before the extension experiment (chaser 3 states, runner 2).
Source: `Benchmark/results/20261125-1110-full.csv`. Means carry a 95% interval from the standard
error; proportions carry a 95% Wilson score interval. All of these figures are generated by
`tools/benchmark/summarize.py` into `summary.md`; this file is the reading of them.

## Does either side's architecture change how the game plays? (n = 60 per side)

| side | brain | chaser win % (95% CI) | mean runners caught | last capture (s) | caught all: n | time to catch all (s) |
| --- | --- | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| chaser | BT | 93.3 (84.1–97.4) | 3.93 | 21.91 ± 3.09 | 56 | 22.61 ± 3.22 |
| chaser | FSM | 91.7 (81.9–96.4) | 3.90 | 20.94 ± 3.42 | 55 | 20.44 ± 3.22 |
| runner | BT | 90.0 (79.9–95.3) | 3.88 | 22.66 ± 3.63 | 54 | 22.57 ± 3.53 |
| runner | FSM | 95.0 (86.3–98.3) | 3.95 | 20.20 ± 2.80 | 57 | 20.55 ± 2.91 |

Two time columns, because they answer different questions. **Last capture** averages every match
in which anything was caught, including matches that ran out of time with two runners still free —
and such a match contributes a flatteringly early time, since the clock stops at whoever was caught
last. **Time to catch all** averages only the matches that ended with every runner caught, which is
what the phrase means. The two differ by under a second here, but in opposite directions per side,
so the distinction is worth keeping rather than assuming.

Fisher's exact test on the win counts (two-sided): chaser BT 56/60 vs FSM 55/60, p = 1.000; runner
BT 54/60 vs FSM 57/60, p = 0.491. Neither is significant.

The data does not separate the two architectures on play outcome. That is a statement about what
this experiment can show, not a proof of equivalence: with chasers winning 90–100% the design has
almost no power to detect a difference in win rate (see the ceiling note at the end), so the
non-significant result is weak evidence on its own. Time to catch all, which has real spread, tells
the same story and is the column to rely on.

`fsm_bt` (83.3%) against `fsm_fsm` (100%) is the widest apparent gap, but at n = 30 the Wilson
intervals run 66–93% and 89–100% and overlap, this is the widest of four comparisons made at once,
and the gap disappears in the 2 × 2 grouping above. Treat the per-matchup spread as noise.

## Decision cost per tick (ms)

| side | brain | mean | median of per-match max | worst single tick |
| --- | --- | --: | --: | --: |
| chaser | BT | 0.0093 | 0.135 | 1.5 |
| chaser | FSM | 0.0105 | 0.150 | 0.7 |
| runner | BT | 0.0200 | 1.376 | 32.9 |
| runner | FSM | 0.0217 | 1.356 | 37.8 |

These include the shared action work a tick performs (`MoveTo`, `NavMesh.SamplePosition`), which
dominates: both architectures call identical action code. Runner ticks cost about twice a chaser's
because of Decoy clone spawning, and the worst single ticks (30 ms+) are one-off spikes of that
kind, not a property of either architecture. Isolating selection cost needs the separate EditMode
benchmark.

## Ceiling effect to note

Chasers win 90–100% in every matchup: 4 chasers against 4 runners with no rescue mechanic, so the
count of free runners only falls. Win rate therefore has little room to discriminate anything, and
that is a limit of the experiment, not a finding about the architectures: at these rates, 60
matches a side could not detect even a fairly large real difference. Anything read off the win-rate
column should be read as "this experiment cannot tell", not as "the two are the same". Time to
catch all varies properly (sd ≈ 12 s on a ≈ 21 s mean) and is the usable outcome metric.
